Is College Still Worth It?
Since 1960, college has transformed from an opportunity for a minority of high school graduates into a near-default pathway for millions.

Enrollment has expanded dramatically, especially among women, while tuition costs have risen to unprecedented levels.

As higher education expanded, so did its risks. Not every student finishes, and not every degree leads to the same financial outcome.

This interactive article explores six decades of data to examine whether college still offers a reliable pathway to economic opportunity — and for whom that promise holds true.

1. College Enrollment Over Time
Since 1960, college enrollment among recent high school graduates has steadily increased.

Enrollment rose from about 40% to nearly 68% for women, and from about 53% to around 60% for men.

Over time, women began enrolling at higher rates than men — a major shift in higher education participation.

The trend reflects a broader transformation: college has increasingly become the dominant pathway after high school.

2. Geographic Distribution
Across the timeline, the map becomes darker, indicating overall growth in college enrollment nationwide.

Several large states such as California, Texas, and New York consistently show the highest enrollment levels.

This suggests that while participation increased across the country, major population centers remain the largest hubs of college enrollment.

3. Equity in Access
Percentage of recent high school completers enrolled in college, by race/ethnicity: 1960–2022.

Across the entire period, all racial and ethnic groups show clear increases in college enrollment.

Among them, Black students experienced the steepest growth, reflecting expanding access to higher education over time.

Best-fit trend lines are shown to highlight the long-term patterns and smooth year-to-year variation.

4. Accelerating Costs
Tuition has increased dramatically over time.

When adjusted for inflation, costs rise steadily from the 1980s to the late 2010s, with a slight drop after 2019.

However, raw tuition costs grow at a nearly exponential rate, especially for four-year institutions.

This rapid growth highlights the increasing financial burden placed on students and families.

5. Financing the Future

Over time, the structure of aid has shifted.

Federal grants gradually declined after 2009, while state, local, and institutional grants increased steadily.

Despite these changes, the percentage of students taking out student loans has remained high. Loans peaked around 2009 and stayed high throughout the following decade.

The pattern shows how rising college costs increasingly rely on financial aid and debt to bridge the gap.

6. The Risk of Non-Completion
Graduation rate within 150% of normal time (2000–2019).

This visualization focuses on two-year institutions such as community colleges, vocational schools, and technical colleges.

Overall, only about 30% of students graduate within three years, and this low rate appears across racial groups.

Public institutions also show lower graduation rates than private schools.

Low completion rates highlight an important risk: many students take on the cost of college without finishing a degree.

7. Return on Investment
Earnings data from the FiveThirtyEight Recent Graduates dataset.

A college degree does not produce a single economic outcome.

Financial returns vary dramatically by major. Some fields lead to strong earnings, while others carry greater financial risk.

What you study matters. College may still deliver strong economic returns overall — but those returns depend heavily on field of study.

A More Complex Equation
Over the past sixty years, college participation has expanded enormously.

More students attend than ever before.

But that growth has unfolded alongside rising costs, unequal access, growing reliance on financial aid, uncertain completion, and highly variable financial outcomes.

Higher education remains powerful. It can still open doors to opportunity, mobility, and long-term economic gain.

Yet it is no longer a simple or uniform pathway.

Instead, college today is shaped by trade-offs — between access and affordability, participation and completion, enrollment and return.

The question is no longer simply “Is college worth it?”

It is: for whom, under what conditions, and in which fields does the promise truly hold?
